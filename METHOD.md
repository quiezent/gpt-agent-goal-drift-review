# How I investigated completion failures

I selected the latest 10 eligible logical chats in each of three projects by local `updated_at` metadata. Here, latest means most recently active, not most recently created. I included archived chats and excluded internal subagents from the 30-chat selection. The selection was captured around 23:04 Malaysia time on 6 October 2026. Some metadata projections lag retained activity, so I preserve the selection rule rather than pretend it perfectly timestamps every last action.

I used read-only app metadata, turn projections and local retained session records. That differs from the earlier trading-bias project's stated method, which used supported task readers and named source files without inspecting private app databases or session files. I do not carry its method claim into this investigation. I read its README, method, report opening and agent-completion follow-up as related work at commit [`1c06de6f1f666b0f43facf99ca36e0eeb4b17421`](https://github.com/quiezent/gpt-trading-decision-bias-review/tree/1c06de6f1f666b0f43facf99ca36e0eeb4b17421).

Across the selected chats I found 34 retained rollout files, 1,784 indexed turns, 1,853 distinct raw task-start records and 569 compaction records. Indexed turns and raw starts differ because rollover, continuation and rewritten histories are not fully represented in the current projection. The counts describe exposure; they are not failure rates or independent trials. One selected V20 Researcher history is abbreviated and has no indexed turns, which limits its assessment.

I extracted and reviewed available user and assistant histories, then inspected tools, results, later corrections and ending boundaries around specific incidents. Parallel project reviewers helped inspect the three groups. I checked 116 cited source references against their retained record lines and timestamps. I did not rerun every historical command, independently reproduce every code defect, query a broker to verify fills, or run a controlled model benchmark.

My central question was whether the task reached its commissioned observable result. A completed turn is not necessarily a completed job. Likewise, an old `inProgress` row is not proof of an active loop. I compared the request, the work actually delivered, the next available action, the reason given for stopping and the subsequent disposition. I looked for a real completed session, a generated evaluation reply, an installed public operator or a commissioned repair, rather than counting tests and reviews as interchangeable evidence.

I keep direct visible quotations separate from shortened excerpts and described tool results. The public evidence labels those records accordingly. A model's later admission supports an observable chronology; it does not disclose or prove an underlying neural mechanism. The source line numbers and SHA-256 hashes refer to private retained JSONL records, including their line terminators, rather than to the shortened public excerpts. The owner can compare them to retained originals. Public readers receive selected evidence, not independently replayable full histories. Account identifiers, personal workstation paths and unnecessary order quantities and prices are omitted from this export.

I distinguish the parent agent, its helpers, the model under evaluation and the original author of inherited code. Historical context metadata and explicit helper requests establish the first two where available. They do not establish the last from a repair message alone. Current titles or model labels are insufficient for historical attribution.

Twenty case records cover related and sometimes overlapping episodes. Several IBKR cases concern one trainer or evaluator; several cloud cases concern the same failed evaluation campaign. I do not count each preparatory repair, each chat or each subagent as a separate experiment. A finite repair chain can demonstrate nonprogress without establishing a literally endless loop.

I found 26 distinct indexed failed turns across 16 chats with recorded causes: 10 capacity errors, nine usage-limit errors, two routing timeouts, two stream disconnections, two blocked requests whose reason is unspecified, and one revoked authentication token. These explain some abrupt stops without attributing them to model indecision. I also exclude explicit user stops, superseded assignments, justified HOLD decisions, genuine uncertainty boundaries and adopted controls from automatic failure attribution. Successful repairs remain in the cases.

The clearest compaction-associated task drop occurred 17 seconds after compaction, but its original deployment instructions remained readable. The compacted summary itself is encrypted. That establishes an immediate failure to continue the broader job; it does not establish which summary content existed or that memory was erased. Other context errors concern the scope and precedence of information that was still present.

The earlier trading-bias report did not establish an always-false PnL prerequisite in its inspected portfolio paths. The newer IBKR trainer record reports a different frozen producer/consumer conjunction. I preserve that boundary: the newer finding is documentary evidence about that training/execution path, not a retroactive reproduction of the earlier project's illustrative claim.

This repository publishes a bounded documentary assessment. It cannot establish unseen history, hidden reasoning, training-induced causation, hypothetical trading profit, or a reliability ranking between models. Its useful conclusion is narrower: I can identify where the requested result was replaced, where the ordinary workflow failed, what model was active on the cited turn, and whether recovery followed.
